Executive summary

West Ham United is entering a period of high pressure. Relegation in May 2026 ended 14 consecutive Premier League seasons. The club now plays in the Championship, has had four different head coaches since the start of 2024, and a further change was reported in November 2026. These are exactly the conditions in which, as the lecture showed, unethical decisions become more likely.

We identify three contextual factors: (C1) promotion and relegation pressure, (C2) rivalry and media scrutiny, and (C3) club governance and leadership structure. We also identify three individual factors of the sport manager: (I1) risk attitude, (I2) moral standards and ethical leadership, and (I3) education and time in office. A multi-level interaction matrix shows three things:

  • Context raises the stakes. It does not make managers unethical, but it increases the pressure and the temptation.
  • Individual characteristics decide how managers respond to that pressure.
  • Governance decides whether bad responses are stopped. It is also the only one of the six factors the club fully controls.

The strongest interactions are:

  • Relegation pressure × risk attitude: a manager who feels they are losing becomes more willing to gamble on promotion.
  • Relegation pressure × moral standards: decisions on returning injured players and on betting sponsorship.
  • Governance × ethical leadership: each reinforces the other.

We recommend (1) integrity and financial guardrails for the promotion push, (2) an independent player-welfare and data protocol, and (3) leadership selection, development and succession. Together they should protect West Ham’s finances, its players and its reputation while it tries to return to the Premier League.

1Purpose, definitions and approach

Sport manager. In this paper, the sport manager means the club’s senior sporting decision-makers: the head coach and the executive responsible for football operations (director of football or technical director), who report to the chief executive and the board.

Ethical decision-making. Decisions that respect the rules and the integrity of competition, protect the welfare and rights of players and staff, and consider all stakeholders. In other words, not only whether we win but how we win.

Framework. The literature review presented in the lecture (99 studies) places ethical issues at three levels: individual, organisational (meso) and industry. Most studies look at one level only, although the levels are expected to interact. We therefore choose factors from all three levels and analyse how they interact (table 1). We also use the ethical domains identified in the review, adding the two issues discussed in class:

  • Use of player data

2West Ham United in November 2026: the facts

  • Relegation. West Ham finished 18th in the 2025/26 Premier League with 39 points and was relegated on the final day (24 May 2026). It was the club’s first relegation since 2011.
  • Coaching turnover. David Moyes left in May 2024, followed by Julen Lopetegui (until January 2025), Graham Potter (until September 2025) and Nuno Espírito Santo. Several outlets reported Nuno’s dismissal on 3 November 2026, although reports conflict.
  • Ownership. The club has been controlled since 2010 by David Sullivan and the Gold family (David Gold died in 2023), with Daniel Křetínský a minority shareholder since 2021. Karren Brady has been vice-chair since 2010.
  • Betting sponsorship. A betting company has sponsored the front of the shirt since 2015 (Betway, then BoyleSports from 2025). After relegation the club kept BoyleSports for 2026/27, because the Premier League’s ban on front-of-shirt gambling sponsors does not apply in the Championship. The 2025 deal was signed while a club player was under investigation over alleged betting breaches.
  • Regulation. The Football Governance Act 2025 (Royal Assent on 21 July 2025) created an Independent Football Regulator with a licensing regime for the top five tiers of English men’s football, including the Championship.

3Three contextual factors

C1 · Promotion and relegation pressure (industry level)

What it is. In England’s open league system, relegation causes a sharp drop in revenue. This is cushioned by parachute payments that shrink over time, which gives the club a limited window to win promotion back.

Ethical risk. The lecture named promotion and relegation as situations with more unethical behaviour than usual. For West Ham this means four temptations:

  • Overspending on wages, and stretching the EFL’s financial rules, to buy a quick return.
  • Rushing injured players back to the pitch in a demanding 46-game season.
  • Putting medical staff in a conflict between the player’s health and the team’s needs.
  • Choosing commercial partners for money rather than values. Keeping a betting sponsor after relegation is legal, but it is a debatable decision under financial pressure.

Effect on performance. Such decisions can bring a short-term boost. However, sanctions, recurring injuries and financial distress endanger performance over the following seasons.

C2 · Rivalry and media scrutiny (industry level)

What it is. West Ham’s rivalry with Millwall and its London derbies make some matches far more important than others. Coverage by the media and well-connected journalists is constant, and every player, coach and employee represents the club in public.

Ethical risk. Research cited in the lecture shows that people in rival and highly competitive situations are more likely to break rules (Kilduff et al., 2016). Media pressure also creates a risk that sensitive information leaks. The lecture’s example was a player’s medical condition becoming public even after the club asked the media not to report it.

Effect on performance. Rivalry and media pressure encourage emotional, short-term decisions, such as sacking a coach after a derby defeat. They also distract the squad, and leaks damage players’ trust in the club.

C3 · Club governance and leadership structure (organisational level)

What it is. Governance is the system of rules, measures and monitoring that steers an organisation and the people in it. Good governance has four dimensions (lecture; Geeraert, 2015): transparency, democratic decision-making, accountability and social responsibility.

Effect on performance. When accountability is unclear, recruitment and coaching decisions become inconsistent. Each new coach brings a new playing style and new demands for players, which wastes resources.

4Three individual factors of the sport manager

I1 · Risk attitude

Sport managers differ in how much risk they are willing to take. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) shows that people become risk-seeking when they perceive themselves to be losing. A freshly relegated club is a textbook case of that perception. A risk-seeking manager is therefore more likely to “gamble” on promotion: expensive short-term signings, big wage commitments, or pressure on injured key players to play.

I2 · Moral standards and ethical leadership

Managers range from a “win at all costs” attitude to high moral standards in which how the club wins matters. Ethical leadership combines being a moral person (honest, trustworthy, caring) with being a moral manager who actively promotes ethics through role modelling, communication, rewards and discipline (Treviño et al., 2000). Because leaders are role models, if the boss behaves unethically, staff feel free to do the same (Brown et al., 2005; lecture). The lecture’s example of a Liverpool manager who supported staff well beyond his own role shows the opposite effect: a culture in which misconduct would feel like letting everyone down.

I3 · Education and time in office

Upper echelons theory holds that an organisation reflects the characteristics of its top managers (Hambrick & Mason, 1984). The evidence presented in the lecture for sport managers shows two patterns:

  • Education has a positive effect on sporting success.
  • Time in office has a positive effect at first and turns negative after a turning point, as managers become committed to old approaches and less open to change (Hambrick & Fukutomi, 1991).

West Ham shows both extremes. The ownership and board leadership have been in place for about 16 years, possibly beyond the turning point (our interpretation). Sporting leaders, by contrast, have had very short tenures and have rarely reached the positive phase.

Influence runs both ways

The matrix mainly shows how the context shapes the manager. But managers also shape their context:

  • I1 → C1: overspending on a promotion gamble increases the financial pressure in the following seasons if promotion fails, creating a vicious circle.
  • I2 → C2: managers who attack rivals or referees in public escalate the rivalry and the media pressure on everyone in the club.
  • I2 and I3 → C3: leaders design and live the governance culture. Long-serving leaders in particular decide how much transparency and challenge the club accepts.

Manager profiles under pressure

Combining the two strongest individual factors, I1 and I2, gives four typical profiles. Contextual pressure (C1, C2) pushes managers towards the left-hand column. Good governance (C3) and careful selection keep them in the top row.

Key insights

  1. Context amplifies. Relegation, rivalry and media pressure do not create unethical managers, but they make the same manager more likely to cut corners.
  2. Individuals decide. Under the same pressure, a “bold builder” and a “gambler” take very different decisions. Who is selected matters.
  3. Governance is the lever. C3 is the only factor the club fully controls, and it moderates all the others. Our recommendations therefore start there.

6Three recommendations and their expected outcomes

R1 · Integrity and financial guardrails for the promotion push

AddressesC1 × I1 (gambling on promotion), C1 × I2 (sponsor choice), C3 (accountability)
Action
  • Set a board-approved promotion budget with a wage-to-revenue ceiling safely below the EFL’s financial limits.
  • Prepare a written plan for the scenario of no promotion in 2026/27 or 2027/28.
  • Create a Risk & Integrity Committee that includes an independent non-executive director. Every major transfer, contract and sponsorship above an agreed threshold needs its review.
  • Adopt a sponsorship-ethics policy that screens all partners. Prepare to move away from betting on the shirt by the end of the current deal.
ResponsibilityThe CEO proposes; the board approves; the finance director and the independent director lead the committee.
TimingCommittee in place before the January 2027 transfer window; policy and scenario plan by March 2027.
Expected outcomeSustainable investment instead of a gamble, compliance with the EFL rules and the new regulator, and more trust from fans and sponsors (good governance leads to trust and credibility).
KPIsWage-to-revenue ratio within target · zero breaches of financial rules · 100% of sponsors screened · fan trust score in the annual survey

R2 · Independent player-welfare and data protocol

AddressesC1 × I2 (rushed returns to play), C2 × I2 (leaks and privacy), conflicts of interest for medical staff
Action
  • Medical decisions. Return-to-play and concussion decisions are taken by the medical department alone and are documented. The head coach and the sporting executive cannot overrule them. The chief medical officer reports to an independent board member, which removes the doctor’s conflict between the player and the team.
  • Health data. Access to health data is limited by role and requires the player’s informed consent. The club receives only a player’s fitness to play, not diagnoses. This follows UK data-protection law (UK GDPR).
  • Communication. A media policy for players and staff: no disclosure of medical information, a reminder on conduct and communication before rivalry matches, and a confidential whistleblowing channel.
ResponsibilityThe chief medical officer and the head of legal draft the protocol; the board approves it; the communications director owns the media policy.
TimingDraft by March 2027; full implementation from the start of the 2027/28 season.
Expected outcomeFewer recurring injuries and better availability over 46-game seasons, protected privacy and reputation, and higher player trust in the club.
KPIsRe-injury rate · days lost to injury · leaks of medical information (target: 0) · players’ trust in medical staff (annual survey)

R3 · Leadership selection, development and succession

AddressesI2 (ethical leadership), I3 (education and time in office), C3 × I3 (selection and renewal), C2 × I1 (reactive sackings)
Action
  • Selection. Appoint the head coach and the director of football against explicit criteria for competence and ethical leadership: qualifications, track record and values, with a structured integrity interview and references.
  • Accountability. Split responsibilities clearly: the director of football owns the recruitment strategy and the head coach owns team performance. Both work to three-year plans with annual reviews instead of reactive sackings.
  • Development. Leadership and ethics training, and a code of conduct with clear expectations of senior staff as role models.
  • Renewal. A board renewal and succession plan, with regular director reviews and independent directors, so that long tenure does not pass the turning point.
ResponsibilityThe chair and the board (appointments); the CEO; the HR director (training and succession).
TimingSelection criteria applied immediately to the current head-coach appointment (November–December 2026); training from early 2027; board renewal plan by June 2027, published in the annual report.
Expected outcomeA consistent sporting strategy, fewer leadership changes, and a culture in which staff copy ethical behaviour. This should improve sporting performance over time.
KPIsHead-coach tenure (target: at least two full seasons) · unplanned leadership changes · staff ethics-climate survey · share of senior staff trained · points-per-game trend

7Limitations

  • Public information only. We had no access to internal governance documents, medical procedures or financial plans, so the governance ratings and matrix strengths are our reasoned judgement.
  • A fast-moving situation. The status of the head coach and the club’s league position were changing at the time of writing.
  • Little evidence at industry level. As the lecture’s review showed, few studies examine industry-level factors or how the levels interact. Some of the links we describe are therefore plausible propositions rather than proven effects.

8Conclusion

West Ham’s future performance depends not only on talent and money, but on how its managers decide under pressure. Relegation, rivalry and media scrutiny will keep raising the stakes. Whether that pressure leads to gambling and cutting corners, or to bold but responsible decisions, depends on the people in charge and on the governance around them. By setting financial and integrity guardrails, protecting player welfare and data, and selecting and developing ethical leaders, West Ham can pursue promotion and protect its sporting integrity at the same time.
